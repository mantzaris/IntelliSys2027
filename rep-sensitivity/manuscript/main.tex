\begin{abstract}
% BEGIN AUTO: abstract
Repeated reports can help information survive communication, but can also change how a decision maker weighs it. We study these effects in a controlled benchmark for selecting the best feasible candidate schedule and a separate rule-based workflow task. Frozen worker reports distinguish repeated presentation from fresh computation and new evidence. Paired worlds test correct responses to consequential changes and correct stability under harmless edits. A held-out evaluation of Qwen3-14B and Phi-4 uses 192 independent base pairs shared across models, 27,648 decision trajectories, and 55,296 model calls. On scheduling, exact repetition changes accuracy by -0.17 percentage points for Qwen3 and -1.09 for Phi-4, with 95\% base-cluster intervals of [-2.21, +1.74] and [-3.17, +1.00]. Corrections and regressions largely offset, and the direct hierarchy interaction is uncertain. An audit of 6,336 development and validation summaries locates numerical objectives becoming Boolean values in raw manager output. A six-pair diagnostic separates missing information from failures to apply a uniquely supported decision. A separate 48-pair mechanism study further tests repetition targets and access to original reports. The results support separate measurement of representation fidelity, decision sufficiency, and final accuracy, while limiting conclusions to the tested tasks, models, and structured interface.
% END AUTO: abstract
\keywords{language-model agents, repetition, hierarchical aggregation, information preservation, behavioral evaluation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A manager coordinating language-model agents often receives overlapping reports.
Some repeat an observation. Others use similar wording for a different candidate,
constraint, or version. The manager must preserve the distinctions that matter,
especially when the next decision maker receives only its summary.

This problem arises even when the original reports are correct. In a scheduling
team, a worker may report that a plan finishes quickly while another finds that
it exceeds machine capacity. Repeating the favorable report adds emphasis without
making the plan feasible. Yet repetition could also help a useful number survive
a summary boundary. The practical question is when repeated presentation improves
the preservation or application of information, and when it distracts from a
requirement that changes the decision.

Existing work already studies evidence duplication, source dependence, and
hierarchical communication \cite{sybil,copies,scaling}. Distributed-information
benchmarks also show that agent teams can fail to discover important facts
\cite{hidden}. We examine a more restricted setting. All required findings are
initially delivered. A flat decision maker reads them directly, while a
hierarchical one receives summaries from two intermediate managers. Correct
answers follow from executable constraints, allowing us to follow information
from its original report to the final decision.

The study asks how repetition affects accuracy, which facts lose their value or
scope, when that loss prevents a justified decision, and whether the model applies
sufficient information correctly. A missing completion time may be irrelevant
if only one candidate is feasible, while a model can choose incorrectly even
when all required comparisons are available.

We contribute a controlled candidate-selection benchmark, paired tests of
consequential and harmless changes, and a trace-based separation of representation
fidelity, decision sufficiency, and final accuracy. The main evaluation covers
192 independent base pairs shared across two models. Its scheduling results do
not reproduce the large benefit seen in a small pilot. Corrections and regressions
largely offset, and the direct repetition-by-hierarchy interaction is uncertain.
A separate numerical audit and completed information-delivery diagnostics show
why preserving a fact and applying it correctly require separate evaluation.
These findings guide interface assessment without prescribing repetition as a
universal remedy or failure source.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Repeated evidence and fresh computation.}
Gao et al. \cite{copies} study restated observations in controlled logs, web
documents, and agent-written communication. Their experiments already include
summaries and hierarchical protocols. The agent-team aggregator reads a roster
and the full log. Our main root instead receives intermediate summaries and a
value-free scope registry, and must combine mandatory constraints with numerical
objectives. The distinction concerns complementary information under restricted
access, rather than the discovery of evidence double counting. Bara \cite{sybil}
separates report multiplicity from evidence ancestry and correlated extraction
errors. These distinctions motivate tracking copies and verification executions
separately.

Whole-prompt repetition can improve non-reasoning models in other settings
\cite{promptrepeat}. Our treatment selectively repeats a report within a larger
evidence set and may alter what an intermediate manager forwards. It therefore
does not directly test or contradict whole-prompt repetition. Self-consistency
samples additional reasoning paths before aggregating answers
\cite{selfconsistency}. Multiagent debate exchanges separately generated responses
\cite{debate}, with benefits that depend on protocol and resource comparisons
\cite{smit}. These methods add computation. Copying an accepted report does not.
Likewise, fresh execution on the same source does not establish independent
evidence. Work on intrinsic self-correction further distinguishes repeated
reasoning from access to external feedback \cite{selfcorrect}.

\paragraph{Organization and distributed information.}
Kim et al. \cite{scaling} examine interactions between agent architecture and task
structure. We use version 3 of that preprint without importing results from earlier
versions. HiddenBench \cite{hidden} studies failures to explore information held
by different agents and evaluates structured communication. Our replay starts
after essential reports have been supplied, isolating later aggregation from
information discovery. The MAST taxonomy \cite{mast} covers a wider set of
coordination and execution failures. Distributed fact recovery under
misinformation \cite{distributed} addresses another boundary. A false report is
not the same intervention as a legitimate change in the world. The main replay
here contains correct reports in both worlds.

\paragraph{Information fidelity and behavioral evaluation.}
Summarization research distinguishes source faithfulness from factual correctness
\cite{maynez}. FRANK further separates predicate, entity, and circumstance errors
\cite{frank}. Our typed scope fields make analogous errors executable to score,
while the task supplies an additional criterion of decision sufficiency.
CheckList \cite{checklist} motivates behavioral tests beyond aggregate accuracy,
and PAWS \cite{paws} shows why lexical overlap does not guarantee equivalent
meaning. Position effects \cite{middle} and evidence metadata \cite{metadata}
provide alternative explanations for changed answers. They caution against
attributing a presentation effect to source counting or a hidden reasoning
process without the corresponding intervention.

\section{Task Setting and Worked Example}
\subsection{Selecting a Feasible Candidate}
The primary task is to select the supplied feasible schedule with the smallest
makespan, the time when its last job finishes. It is not a search for a globally
optimal schedule. Jobs have integer durations and resource demands and cannot be
interrupted. A job starting at $s_j$ with duration $d_j$ occupies the half-open
interval $[s_j,s_j+d_j)$, so finishing at time 2 does not overlap starting at 2.
A candidate is feasible only if precedence, release, resource, deadline, and
downtime requirements all hold where applicable. Ties follow an explicit public
priority order. The answer is \texttt{NONE} when no candidate is feasible.

Writing $F(c)$ for the conjunction of mandatory predicates, $M(c)$ for makespan,
and $\pi(c)$ for tie priority, the required choice is
\begin{equation}
 c^*=\mathop{\arg\min}_{c:F(c)=1}\bigl(M(c),\pi(c)\bigr).
\label{eq:choice}
\end{equation}
The public decision rule in Eq. \ref{eq:choice} first resolves feasibility and then
compares objectives. A favorable objective cannot compensate for a failed
mandatory constraint.

\subsection{Three Jobs, Two Plans, and One Capacity Change}
Jobs A and B each need one unit of machine M for two time units. Job C needs one
unit of machine N for one time unit and follows both A and B. Machine N has
capacity one. Table \ref{tab:example} gives two illustrative schedules. Both
independently implemented benchmark checkers verify the example. It is a
constructed explanation, not a reported model failure.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\caption{Illustrative schedules. Intervals are half-open and all times use the same unit.}
\label{tab:example}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}\toprule
Plan & A on M & B on M & C on N & Makespan\\\midrule
P & $[0,2)$ & $[0,2)$ & $[2,3)$ & 3\\
Q & $[0,2)$ & $[2,4)$ & $[4,5)$ & 5\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At capacity one on M, P fails because A and B overlap. Q is feasible and correct.
At capacity two, P becomes feasible and is the correct, faster choice. The
objective and precedence reports do not change. The consequential difference is
P's resource feasibility.

An objective worker contributes the statement that P's makespan is 3. A resource
worker contributes the separate finding that P violates M's capacity in the first
world. Four presentations of the objective statement still describe one result.
They do not provide four independent checks or establish P's overall feasibility.

Now suppose a manager replaces both objective values with \texttt{true}. At
capacity one, the complete feasibility findings still identify Q as the only
possible choice. The objectives are represented incorrectly, but the decision is
determined. At capacity two, both candidates are feasible and their missing
objective values matter. Finally, if the root selects P after receiving the
capacity-one failure and Q's feasibility, it violates sufficient information.
This example separates a preserved proposition, enough information to decide,
and a correct final decision.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Instances, Claims, and Paired Worlds}
Each base problem has an original and modified world, with every candidate checked
by executable rules. The main scheduling set uses six to eight jobs, three
candidates, and two or three resource types. An event-sweep checker and an
independent integer-time occupancy checker agree on feasibility and objectives.
Controlled alterations include fast feasible and infeasible candidates, ties,
and cases without a feasible candidate. Names, presentation order, and public tie
priority are randomized independently of the answer.

Scheduling mutations change capacities, resource identities, or precedence.
A separate workflow task covers quantifiers, conditions, and explicit version
applicability using deterministic Boolean requirements and candidate objective
values. The task defines version authority explicitly. Its result is secondary.
Within each task family, half the main pairs
change the correct decision and half preserve it. All variants of one base stay
in the same split. Held-out instances reserve graph patterns, parameter
combinations, and rendering templates in addition to new seeds. The small
structural vocabulary remains a limitation on generalization.

Replay worker reports are generated by deterministic checks and frozen before
model inference. They are not new language-model worker executions. A claim binds
its value to a task, candidate, entity, predicate, units, condition, and version.
Source identifiers, parent reports, and verification execution identifiers record
ancestry separately. Similar wording for different candidates or resources is
therefore not treated as an equivalent claim. The implementation also separates
job precedence, verification tasks, organization, communication access, and report
ancestry graphs.

Changed-world pairs regenerate affected reports consistently, so their initial
reports are correct. Corrupted reports, autonomous worker errors, and genuine
new observations are different interventions. They are not pooled into the main
replay. The common record format makes controlled near duplication possible, but
a between-world accuracy change alone is not evidence that two within-input
claims were falsely merged.

\subsection{Presentation, Access, and Aggregation Policy}
The baseline displays each required finding once. Exact repetition adds three
copies of one selected report. The paraphrase arm adds three fixed-template
restatements with the same value, scope, and ancestry. The selected report thus
has one or four total presentations. All essential findings remain available.
A prespecified rotation counterbalances the target across candidate and task
families, preferring a claim unchanged across paired worlds. Selection does not
use model outcomes or guarantee a decisive finding.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{aggregation-architecture.pdf}
\caption{Flat and hierarchical replay. Copies add presentations, not evidence sources or worker executions.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The access difference in Figure \ref{fig:architecture} is the central
organizational intervention. A flat root reads the full report set in one model
call. Two intermediate managers in the hierarchy receive fixed partitions and
summarize them for a root, for three calls in total. Copies remain with their
original report's branch. The hierarchical root receives summaries and a registry
of required scopes and ancestry, without the original assertion values or raw
reports. The registry is orchestration metadata, not an oracle answer.

Ordinary synthesis, a required-task checklist, and scope-aware consolidation share
the same evidence and decision rule. The checklist explicitly asks for mandatory
coverage before optimization. Scope-aware consolidation additionally asks the
model to merge equivalent claims, retain differences in entity, condition, or
version, and expose unresolved conflicts. It receives no extra truth labels.
Because it also adds instructions, an effect cannot be assigned solely to
provenance handling.

All policies use a common structured output contract. Managers emit scoped
assertions and unresolved keys. Roots emit candidate assessments, a choice,
probabilities, and supporting claims. Even ordinary synthesis is thus assisted
by a feasibility/objective scaffold. Guided decoding enforces JSON structure but
does not require numerical types specifically at objective keys. The analysis
scores explicit emitted values and decisions, not private reasoning.

fixed. Sampling uses temperature 0.3, top-$p$ 0.95, a 12,288-token context limit,
a 2,304-token output cap, and batches of up to 32. Appendix
\ref{app:reproducibility} records versions and the checkpointed hardware
continuation.

\subsection{Outcomes, Pairing, and Uncertainty}
Exact agreement with the deterministic answer is the primary outcome. A completed
malformed or truncated response counts as an operational error, including a failed
manager response even if the root happens to choose correctly. Accepted failures
are not resampled. Request hashes include the trial and stochastic-generation
identity. The completed main archive contains all planned accepted calls, with
no unfinished clusters.

For each model, we average accuracy within each base over the relevant worlds,
policies, organizations, and generations. The repetition effect is the mean
within-base difference
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_r=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
 \left(\bar Y_{b,r}-\bar Y_{b,\mathrm{single}}\right).
\label{eq:effect}
\end{equation}
Positive values in Eq. \ref{eq:effect} mean an accuracy benefit. The original
repetition-harm definition uses error and has the opposite sign. The hierarchy
interaction directly subtracts the flat repetition effect from the hierarchical
effect, averaging exact and paraphrased repetition. Lower hierarchical accuracy
alone does not establish amplification.

Paired-world success requires both world answers to be correct. It measures
appropriate responsiveness for consequential edits and correct stability for
harmless edits. The predeclared contrast compares repetition effects on these
two outcomes directly. Two further contrasts compare scope-aware consolidation
with the checklist, on accuracy for repeated consequential cases and on retention
of changed claims in hierarchical consequential cases. The main scheduling
population is primary, and workflow, subgroup, calibration, and transition
analyses are secondary or exploratory as identified.

We use 10,000 percentile bootstrap draws of whole base-problem clusters,
stratified by mutation family and decision-change status \cite{clusterbootstrap}.
Each draw retains all worlds, conditions, policies, and generations. Models are
reported separately, with pointwise 95\% intervals. These are not simultaneous
intervals or adjusted significance tests. No confirmatory $p$-values are reported.
The manifest requires Holm adjustment across six primary contrasts if such tests
are added. An interval spanning zero does not establish equivalence. The six-base
root diagnostic uses counts because its strata cannot support useful intervals.

\section{Information Preservation and Decision Sufficiency}
\label{sec:preservation}
\subsection{Where Numerical Information Changes}
The pipeline audit follows original typed reports, manager inputs, raw generated
summaries, parsed summaries, and actual root inputs. It covers 60 independent
pilot and validation bases, 3,168 hierarchical trajectories, and 6,336 manager
summaries. These are different denominators. No field changes were found between
the checked raw manager output, parser output, and root delivery. Numerical
objectives becoming Boolean \texttt{true} were already present in raw model
responses.

The prompts require numerical objectives, but a common assertion schema permits
both Boolean and numerical values without conditioning the value type on the
scope key. A syntactically valid response can therefore lose the objective's
meaning. The audit locates the error in generated communication under this
interface. It does not establish whether a different schema, instruction example,
or model would eliminate it.

\subsection{A Fact Can Be Missing Without Making the Choice Ambiguous}
Representation fidelity asks whether a proposition retains its source value and
scope. Decision sufficiency asks whether all admissible completions of unknown
fields lead to the same answer. Final accuracy asks whether the model selects
the correct candidate in the actual world. None substitutes for the others.

The scheduling support scorer operates on the public report interface. Mandatory
predicates are Boolean and objectives are nonnegative integers. It holds supplied,
uncontested values fixed and enumerates possible feasibility combinations and
objective orderings, including ties. A unique resulting answer is determined by
the visible information. Multiple answers mean the input does not uniquely
justify a choice. The scorer does not fill gaps with evaluator truth or infer
unseen schedule details from hidden constraints.

Contradictory and ill-typed fields are marked separately. The strict representation
has no consistent completion if it contains such fields. A complementary support
analysis treats only those disputed or invalid fields as unknown, while retaining
uncontested values. It can establish that the choice is unchanged under every
admissible replacement, as in the sole-feasible-candidate example. This does not
make the invalid representation correct or resolve the disputed facts.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{Implications for Evaluating Agent Communication}
The main results favor a more specific evaluation question than whether
repetition helps. An average can remain almost unchanged while some previously
correct decisions become wrong and others are corrected. Evaluating those
transitions alongside ordinary stochastic variation makes the distinction visible
without equating every answer change with a new instability mechanism.

The communication audit suggests an equally specific engineering target.
A structured output can be syntactically valid while carrying the wrong kind of
value. Scope-dependent type validation is a plausible way to prevent a Boolean
from being accepted at an objective key, but its effect on final decisions has not
been tested here. An interface improvement should be assessed for preserved
propositions, sufficient information, and correct application separately.

The root diagnostic shows why these measures should remain distinct. Some lost
objectives do not affect a uniquely determined choice, and some correct choices
are not justified by the delivered information. A system that is right for an
unsupported reason can fail when the same missing distinction becomes relevant.
Conversely, a faithful summary does not by itself guarantee that the root will
apply a mandatory constraint or choose the best feasible candidate.

\subsection{Boundaries of the Conclusions}
The benchmark is synthetic and deliberately controlled. It measures integration
of complementary requirements rather than deployment-wide failure rates. The
workflow shares the selection structure with scheduling, limiting domain-transfer
claims. Frozen correct reports isolate aggregation but omit the error process
of autonomous workers. A small fresh-verification diagnostic is summarized in the
appendix and does not establish independence of worker errors.

Organization changes both input access and the number of model calls. The main
result therefore concerns the tested organizational package. Incomplete broader
call-matched, length, ancestry, and live-worker controls prevent stronger claims
about those components. The completed raw-access comparison helps probe the
summary boundary but also changes root input length. Uniform repetition increases
length more than selective repetition. Neither contrast alone identifies why a
decision changed.

Both models use one fixed precision, decoding regime, and structured interface.
The common candidate-assessment scaffold narrows the contrast between ordinary
synthesis and the checklist. The scope policy changes instructions as well as
consolidation guidance. Generalization to other model sizes, reasoning modes,
free-form communication, deeper organizations, or feedback loops remains open.

The original main contrasts were frozen before inference, but pilot-inspired
audits and the stochastic-transition analysis are exploratory. Pointwise intervals
across multiple related comparisons are not family-wise significance tests.
The six-pair diagnostic and the 24 scheduling pairs in the mechanism extension
have limited precision. No equivalence margin was prospectively specified for
the fresh results. Trace associations locate observable changes but do not
identify causal mediation or a faithful internal reasoning process.

\section{Conclusion}
% BEGIN AUTO: conclusion
Across two models and 192 independent held-out base pairs, selective repetition has small, uncertain average scheduling effects and does not reproduce the large hierarchical pilot gain. Corrections and regressions can offset, so average accuracy alone does not describe how decisions change. The secondary workflow results and targeted repetition study further limit any claim of a universal benefit. Trace audits identify numerical-to-Boolean transformations in raw summaries, while the root diagnostic separates lost information from errors made despite a uniquely supported choice. These findings support evaluating the complete communication path through three distinct outcomes: faithful propositions, sufficient information, and correct decisions. They motivate interfaces that preserve required values and scopes, with their decision benefit assessed separately.
% END AUTO: conclusion
