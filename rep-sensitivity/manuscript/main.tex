\begin{abstract}
% BEGIN AUTO: abstract
Repeated reports are common in language-model agent systems, but repeating a finding can both preserve it during communication and change how a decision maker uses it. We study these effects with a deterministic benchmark for selecting the best feasible candidate schedule, alongside a separate rule-based workflow task. Frozen worker reports isolate repeated presentation from fresh computation and new evidence. Paired worlds test correct responses to consequential edits and correct stability under harmless changes. Flat aggregation is compared with two intermediate managers whose summaries form the root input. Completed pilot and validation evaluations cover 24 and 36 independent base problems, respectively. On hierarchical scheduling, exact copies improve Qwen3 pilot accuracy from 81.25\% to 94.79\%, but validation effects are -3.47 percentage points for Qwen3 and +1.39 for Phi-4, with both cluster intervals spanning zero. An audit of 6,336 manager summaries locates numerical objectives becoming Boolean values in raw generated summaries, with no subsequent parser or transport changes. This loss does not always make the decision ambiguous, and correct delivery does not guarantee a correct choice. The results motivate separate evaluation of representation fidelity, decision sufficiency, and decision accuracy. Main held-out and intervention results remain pending in this working draft. Conclusions are limited to the evaluated models, tasks, and structured communication interface.
% END AUTO: abstract
\keywords{language-model agents, hierarchical aggregation, repetition, information preservation, behavioral evaluation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A language-model manager may receive several reports that say nearly the same
thing. Some are copies of an existing observation. Others add a constraint, refer
to a different machine, or correct an earlier value. Reliable coordination
requires the manager to preserve these distinctions while using all the
information needed for its decision. Repetition creates a useful tension. It may
help a fact survive summarization, yet it can also give a limited claim more
influence than its relevance warrants.

Consider a team choosing among proposed schedules. One worker reports completion
times, another checks machine capacity, and another checks dependencies. Repeating
that one plan is fast does not establish that it is feasible. Equally, removing
every similar sentence could discard the one capacity failure that changes the
answer. These are integration problems. The reports answer complementary
questions, so a majority vote over their favorable or unfavorable conclusions is
not a valid decision rule.

Research on repeated evidence already distinguishes sources from restatements
\cite{copies,sybil}. Research on distributed information shows that agent teams can
fail to discover or use facts held by their members \cite{hidden}. Our focus is
the transition between these settings. All required worker findings are initially
delivered, but a hierarchical decision maker receives summaries rather than the
original reports. This lets us examine whether a failure occurs during
communication or after the relevant information has arrived.

We investigate three questions. First, how does repeating a report affect
decision accuracy in flat and hierarchical organizations? Second, where do
complete propositions lose their value or scope during aggregation? Third, when
does such a loss actually prevent a justified decision? The distinction in the
third question matters. A missing completion time is important when several
feasible plans compete, but may be irrelevant when only one plan is feasible.

The study provides a deterministic benchmark for candidate selection, paired
worlds with exactly checked changes, and a replay procedure that varies report
presentation without adding evidence. It also provides a trace audit that follows
assertions from the original report to the final model input, and an analysis of
the decisions supported by that input. These components make it possible to
separate representation fidelity, decision sufficiency, and final accuracy.

The completed development evidence illustrates this distinction. A substantial
hierarchical pilot benefit did not consistently carry over to validation.
Numerical objectives sometimes become Boolean values in raw manager summaries,
but repetition does not reliably prevent this loss. Some decisions are also wrong
despite correctly delivered information. Together, these findings locate different
reliability problems without implying a universal rule for repeating reports.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Repeated evidence and additional computation.}
Gao et al. \cite{copies} measure how aggregators count restated observations in
controlled logs, web documents, and agent-written communication. Their experiments
already include summaries and a hierarchy. In their agent-team setting, the
aggregator sees the roster and full log. Our main hierarchy instead removes raw
reports from the root's input and requires it to combine mandatory constraints
with a numerical objective. This is a narrower distinction than claiming that
evidence double counting or hierarchical communication is new. Bara
\cite{sybil} formalizes why report multiplicity and evidence multiplicity differ,
including the possibility of correlated extraction errors.

Repetition can also be beneficial. Leviathan et al. \cite{promptrepeat} evaluate
repetition of the whole input prompt in non-reasoning models. We selectively
repeat a report and track what survives a summary boundary. Neither intervention
is the same as self-consistency, which samples additional reasoning paths and
aggregates their answers \cite{selfconsistency}. Fresh verification is additional
computation. Exact copies are additional presentations of an existing result.
Neither necessarily supplies a new independent observation.

\paragraph{Agent organization and distributed information.}
Multiagent debate can improve answers by exchanging separately generated
responses \cite{debate}, but its benefits depend on the protocol and comparison
budget \cite{smit}. The current version of the scaling study by Kim et al.
\cite{scaling} examines how architectures interact with task structure. We use its
third preprint version and do not combine findings from earlier versions.
HiddenBench \cite{hidden} tests information distributed across agents, including
failures to elicit unshared facts and a structured communication intervention.
Our replay begins after the essential reports have been supplied. This isolates
later aggregation from the problem of discovering missing evidence. The MAST
taxonomy \cite{mast} places such failures within a broader range of coordination
problems. Hi-Agreement \cite{distributed} studies fact recovery in the presence
of misinformation. It motivates keeping incorrect testimony separate from a
legitimate change in the underlying world.

\paragraph{Faithfulness, scope, and presentation.}
Summarization research distinguishes a summary's faithfulness to its source from
its factual correctness \cite{maynez}. We add a task-specific question: does the
delivered information determine the required choice? Behavioral testing with
CheckList \cite{checklist} motivates both meaning-preserving and consequential
changes. PAWS \cite{paws} demonstrates why high word overlap does not establish
semantic equivalence. Position effects in long contexts \cite{middle} and
metadata effects in conflicting retrieved evidence \cite{metadata} provide
alternative explanations for apparent repetition effects. They motivate the
study's separate ordering, length, scope, and provenance controls.

Prior conclusions and role labels are different interventions. Intrinsic
self-correction can fail without external feedback \cite{selfcorrect}, while
identity labels \cite{identitybias} and authority roles \cite{authoritybias} can
influence multiagent debate or evaluation. These studies motivate separate probes
of feedback and relayed reports. A changed answer alone does not reveal a model's
belief about source independence or authority.

\section{Task Setting and a Worked Example}
\subsection{Choosing Among Supplied Schedules}
The primary task is to select the feasible candidate with the smallest makespan,
where makespan is the latest job completion time. The model chooses from a supplied
set of plans. It does not search for a globally optimal schedule.

Jobs have integer durations and resource demands. They run without interruption
on half-open intervals $[s_j,s_j+d_j)$, so a job finishing at time 2 does not
overlap a job starting at time 2. For resource $r$, total simultaneous demand must
satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j:s_j\leq t<s_j+d_j} q_{jr}\leq C_r,
\end{equation}
where $C_r$ is capacity and $q_{jr}$ is job $j$'s demand. A precedence edge from
$u$ to $v$ requires $s_u+d_u\leq s_v$. Releases, deadlines, and resource downtime
add mandatory constraints when present.

Write $F(c)$ for the conjunction of mandatory constraints and $M(c)$ for the
makespan of candidate $c$. The choice is
\begin{equation}
 c^*=\mathop{\arg\min}_{c:F(c)=1}\bigl(M(c),\pi(c)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where the public priority rank $\pi(c)$ resolves ties lexicographically. The
answer is \texttt{NONE} if no supplied candidate is feasible. An attractive
objective cannot compensate for a failed mandatory constraint.

\subsection{An Illustrative Capacity Change}
Consider jobs A and B, each requiring one unit of machine M for two time units.
Job C requires one unit of a different machine N for one time unit and follows
both A and B. Machine N has capacity one. Table \ref{tab:example} gives two
candidate plans. This is an illustrative example checked with both benchmark
verifiers, not an observed model error.

\centering

\label{tab:example}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Plan & A on M & B on M & C on N & Makespan \\
\midrule
P & $[0,2)$ & $[0,2)$ & $[2,3)$ & 3 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

With machine M's capacity equal to one, P is infeasible because A and B overlap.
Q is feasible and is the correct choice. Increasing M's capacity to two makes P
feasible, so P becomes correct. Precedence and makespan reports remain unchanged.
Only the resource finding for P changes.

An objective worker contributes the statement that P's makespan is 3. A resource
worker contributes the separate statement that its peak demand on M is 2 and
whether that meets the applicable capacity. Four exact presentations of the
objective report still describe one computed result. They cannot establish that
P is feasible when capacity is one.

The same example explains decision sufficiency. At capacity one, the feasibility
findings alone identify Q, even if both numerical objectives disappear. At
capacity two, both plans are feasible. If their objective values are missing,
the remaining reports do not determine which is best. A representation error can
therefore be irrelevant to one decision and decisive for another.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Instances, Reports, and Paired Changes}
The generator builds small schedules from valid structures and controlled
alterations. The evaluated schedules use three candidates and two or three
resource types. Candidate identifiers, ordering, and tie priorities are randomized
without consulting a model. An event-sweep checker evaluates each candidate, and
a separately implemented integer-time occupancy checker cross-checks its
feasibility and objective.

Each independent base problem has an original and a modified world. The pair
records the changed fields and both correct answers. A consequential edit changes
the correct choice. A harmless edit leaves it unchanged. Scheduling pairs cover
capacity, resource identity, and precedence changes. A separate deterministic
workflow family covers quantification, conditional rules, and explicit version
selection. Workflow results are reported separately because these edits have a
different task structure.

Workers' findings are typed assertions rendered with fixed templates. The replay
therefore starts with known reports rather than model-generated worker errors.
Each claim identifies its task, candidate, entity, predicate, value, units,
applicability, and version. It also records sources, parent reports, and the
verification execution. Two claims are equivalent only when their meanings and
scopes agree. A different candidate, resource, condition, or effective version can
make similar wording express a different proposition.

The changed-world replay regenerates affected reports consistently. Separate
corrupted-report controls keep the world fixed and introduce a false assertion
with visible evidence that can resolve it. A contradiction is not automatically a
valid objection. Likewise, a newer report has authority only when the task defines
an update rule. These controls must not be pooled with the all-correct-report
replay as if they were the same intervention.

\subsection{Repetition and Organizational Structure}
The baseline presents every required result once. Exact repetition adds three
copies of one selected report. Paraphrasing adds three template-rendered restatements
of the same claim, retaining its value, scope, and ancestry. Thus the selected
report has one or four total presentations. Essential reports are never removed
to make room. A prespecified rotation selects the target, preferring a report
unchanged across paired worlds. This does not guarantee that the selected report
is decisive or favors the correct candidate.

\caption{Flat and hierarchical replay. Copies add presentations, not sources or worker executions.}
\label{fig:architecture}

Figure \ref{fig:architecture} shows the access difference. A flat root reads the
complete report set in one call. In the hierarchy, two managers receive fixed
partitions, summarize their reports, and pass the summaries to a root. The root
receives a registry of required claim scopes but no raw worker reports. This
registry contains descriptions and ancestry, not evaluator truth values. Copies
initially stay in their report's branch. Every policy receives the same available
information within an arm.

Three policies vary the aggregation instructions. Ordinary synthesis asks for a
decision and its explicit supporting findings. A coverage checklist asks the model
to account for each mandatory predicate before comparing objectives. Scope-aware
consolidation additionally asks it to merge equivalent claims while preserving
different entities, versions, conditions, and unresolved conflicts. Even ordinary
synthesis receives the full decision rule. The common output contract also asks
the root for a feasibility and objective assessment of each candidate. This is a
substantial baseline scaffold, so the comparison concerns incremental instructions
within a structured interface.

Managers emit keyed assertions and unresolved keys. Roots additionally emit a
choice, candidate assessments, and probabilities. The study scores these public
outputs, not private reasoning. The JSON decoder enforces syntax and field
structure. It does not enforce all semantic relationships between a scope key and
its value. Section \ref{sec:preservation} examines the consequences of that
distinction.

\subsection{Models, Stages, and Experimental Integrity}
We use Qwen3-14B \cite{qwenmodel} with thinking disabled and Phi-4
\cite{phimodel}. Both run sequentially in bfloat16 without quantization on one
NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU. Sampling uses temperature 0.3 and top-$p$
0.95. The replay has a 12,288-token context limit, a 2,304-token output cap, and
batches of up to 32 requests. Native chat templates and exact model revisions are
fixed. Appendix \ref{app:reproducibility} gives implementation details.

\subsection{Outcomes and Uncertainty}
Exact agreement with the executable answer is the primary outcome. We also
distinguish infeasible selections, feasible but suboptimal selections, and
incorrect \texttt{NONE} or \texttt{INSUFFICIENT} responses. Paired-world success
requires both answers to be correct. On consequential pairs this measures correct
responsiveness, and on harmless pairs it measures correct stability. An answer
flip alone satisfies neither criterion.

For base problem $b$, let $\bar Y_{b,a,r}$ be accuracy averaged over the specified
worlds, policies, and stochastic repetitions in organization $a$ and presentation
condition $r$. We report the accuracy effect
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{a,r}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
       (\bar Y_{b,a,r}-\bar Y_{b,a,\mathrm{single}}).
\end{equation}
Positive values favor repetition. The original error-based repetition-harm
contrast is $-\Delta_{a,r}$. We estimate the hierarchy interaction directly as
$\Delta_{\mathrm{hierarchical},r}-\Delta_{\mathrm{flat},r}$. A lower overall
hierarchical accuracy does not by itself show that hierarchy amplifies repetition.

Uncertainty uses whole base-problem clusters \cite{clusterbootstrap}. Each
bootstrap draw retains the associated worlds, arms, policies, and repetitions.
The displayed exploratory estimates use 10,000 draws stratified by mutation
family and whether the correct decision changes. Models are reported separately.
Intervals are percentile 95\% intervals, not multiplicity-adjusted tests. We make
no confirmatory significance claims from these development analyses. Few clusters
limit precision, and a zero-width interval at a sample ceiling is not evidence of
perfect population performance.

\section{Information Preservation and Decision Sufficiency}
\label{sec:preservation}
\subsection{Locating the Numerical Transformation}
The audit follows the original typed report, actual manager input, raw generated
summary, parsed assertions, serialized summary, and actual root input. It covers
6,336 manager summaries from 3,168 hierarchical trajectories across the completed
pilot and validation. These trajectories come from 60 independent base problems,
with validation problems shared across models.

Numerical objectives becoming Boolean \texttt{true} first appear in the raw
model-generated manager summaries. The audit found no value changes introduced
by parsing, serialization, or root-input assembly, and no disagreement between
raw final choices and their primary scores. It also found no truncated manager
outputs. Within the audited pipeline, this is observed model behavior under the
specified interface, not a downstream transformation defect.

The instructions explicitly require numerical objectives, but the assertion
schema permits either a Boolean or a number at every scope key. Its illustrative
assertion uses \texttt{true}. Thus a syntactically valid answer can replace a
makespan with a truth indicator. The interface permits this semantic error. The
audit does not establish that the example caused it, and the frequency need not
generalize to a schema that enforces a numeric type for objective keys.

The influential pilot pair offers a concrete illustration of the observed path.
All three candidates are feasible and their objectives are 8, 9, and 9. All eight

isolate it from changes in attention, summarization, or final integration.

\subsection{What the Delivered Information Supports}
Representation fidelity asks whether a complete proposition survives with the
same candidate, value, units, condition, and version. Decision sufficiency asks
whether the visible information determines a choice. World accuracy asks whether
the chosen answer matches the executable task. These outcomes can disagree.
A lucky correct choice after a necessary fact is lost is still correct at the
world level, but is not evidence of successful integration of that fact.

The visible-information scorer first identifies missing values, invalid types,
and conflicting assertions. It does not replace them with hidden ground truth.
For a separate decision-support calculation, it holds uncontested visible
assertions fixed and varies unknown or disputed predicates and objectives over
admissible values in the report interface. Boolean predicates can be true or
false, and objectives are nonnegative integers. A decision is determined only if
every admissible completion yields the same answer under the public tie rule.
Being possible under one completion is not enough.

This calculation is an abstraction of the report interface, not an enumeration
of all underlying job schedules. It can conservatively leave a decision unresolved
that additional domain relationships would determine. It also does not certify
that uncontested assertions are true in the world. Contradictions and invalid
representations remain explicitly labeled even when they concern an irrelevant
field and the remaining information already fixes the choice.

\subsection{Observable Failure Locations}
The trace analysis permits several labels per trajectory. A required proposition
can be omitted, emitted with the wrong type or value, or linked to an incompatible
scope. At the decision stage, a selected candidate can violate a delivered
mandatory failure, or be feasible but worse than another feasible candidate.
A correct choice can also be accompanied by erroneous supporting assertions.
Contract failure is a separate operational label.

Keyed assertions and executable outcomes support deterministic scoring of these
cases. An incompatible citation reveals a scope mismatch, but not an internal
transfer of belief. Shared wording alone cannot establish an incorrect semantic
merge. The categories describe public outputs, not a faithful record of internal
reasoning.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{Designing for Facts and Decisions}
Repeating a report and preserving its contents are different operations. The
validation evidence does not justify relying on repetition to preserve numerical
values consistently. A schema with scope-dependent value types is a plausible
engineering response, whose effect on accuracy requires controlled evaluation.

Preservation and correct application are also separate goals. Evaluation should
check both the communicated propositions and the decision rule they support.
This matters for complementary constraints, where several favorable reports
cannot cancel one mandatory failure.

Reporting case-level effects reveals concentration that an average can hide.
The influential pair remains in the pilot analysis. Validation refines the
question from whether repetition helps to which information and interfaces make
it useful, without identifying a cause of non-generalization.

\subsection{Scope of the Evidence}
These synthetic tasks measure controlled candidate selection, not deployment
failure rates. Scheduling and the workflow share a decision structure, limiting
claims of transfer across tasks.

Template reports isolate aggregation while omitting real workers' errors.
Fresh executions using the same model and source can share errors, so additional
computation need not supply independent evidence.

The hierarchy changes access and call count, requiring bypass and call-matched
controls to separate them. Deeper hierarchies and feedback dynamics remain outside
this replay.

The JSON contract and two model configurations constrain generalization.
Allocated batch time describes this implementation, not individual latency,
GPU kernel time, or monetary cost.

The audit is observational and cannot identify causal mediation. Deterministic
preservation is an idealized control, not evidence that an autonomous summarizer
achieves the same fidelity. Small targeted diagnostics require fresh confirmation
before supporting broader mechanism claims.

\section{Conclusion}
% BEGIN AUTO: conclusion
Controlled replay separates repeated presentation from additional evidence. A large hierarchical pilot benefit is followed by smaller, mixed validation effects. Numerical objectives sometimes become Boolean assertions in manager output, but repetition does not reliably prevent this loss. Delivered information can remain sufficient despite a representation error, and a root can decide incorrectly despite a uniquely supported choice. Separating fidelity, sufficiency, and accuracy gives a more informative account of reliability. It also identifies a practical goal for future interventions. Preserve the required propositions, then verify that the decision follows from them.
% END AUTO: conclusion
